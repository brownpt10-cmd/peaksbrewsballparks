Objective To test whether the 2012 conclusions still hold once 15 more years of data are added: that Himalayan experience gives no survival benefit, and that commercial expeditions are no more dangerous than traditional ones. A secondary aim was to compare commercial expeditions run by Nepali operators with those run by Western operators.

Design Retrospective cohort study that rebuilds the original model and then extends it.

Setting Nepalese Himalaya, 1970 to the end of 2025.

Participants 65,398 climbs above base camp by 38,122 climbers. High altitude porters and other hired staff are excluded. The model uses the 37,308 climbs (517 deaths) on the eight Nepalese peaks over 8000 m.

Main outcome Death during the climb.

Results Rebuilt on the original 1970–2010 data, the model reproduced the published estimates (experience OR 1.00; Everest 0.42; Cho Oyu 0.17; summit success 1.49; calendar year 0.97). Through 2025, increased Himalayan experience was still not associated with a change in the odds of death (OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.96 to 1.01, P=0.31). Commercial climbing was associated with 39% lower odds of death relative to non-commercial climbing (0.61, 0.44 to 0.84, P=0.003). In the post-2010 period alone the figure was 56% lower (0.44, 0.27 to 0.71, P=0.001). Over 2010–2025, Western-run expeditions had the lowest odds of death (0.30, 0.15 to 0.61) and Nepali-run expeditions intermediate odds (0.53, 0.32 to 0.87), both relative to non-commercial climbs. Nepali-run expeditions carried roughly twice the odds of Western-run ones (1.8 to 2.1), and 2.4 to 2.6 on Everest. The gap narrowed in 2020–25. Year of expedition was still associated with a significant trend toward reduced odds of death (0.98, 0.97 to 1.00, P=0.022). Deaths clustered on the busiest summit days. Before 2010, experienced climbers turned back more often on those days (interaction OR 0.87 per doubling of experience, P=0.006). After 2010 that pattern was gone. Each hour later on the summit raised the odds of dying on the descent (OR 1.38 per hour, 1.25 to 1.52). The shift to earlier summits (half of summiters on top before 8am after 2010, against under a quarter before) accounts for nearly all of the decrease in descent deaths.

Conclusions Both 2012 conclusions hold, and the commercial finding is now statistically significant. Experience still confers no survival benefit. Commercial expeditions are safer than non-commercial climbs, and the operator matters: Western-run expeditions were the safest, and Nepali-run expeditions were safer than independent climbing but not as safe as Western-run ones.

What is already known on this topic

  • Up to 2010, Himalayan experience was not associated with survival (OR 1.00).
  • Commercial climbs showed 37% lower odds of death, but the confidence interval included no effect (P=0.10).
  • Since 2010, Nepali-owned operators have taken over most of the commercial market.

What this study adds

  • With 15 more years of data, experience still gives no survival benefit to clients.
  • The commercial survival advantage is now statistically significant (OR 0.61 overall, 0.44 after 2010).
  • Western-run commercial expeditions had about half the odds of death of Nepali-run ones, although the gap narrowed in 2020–25.
  • Deaths cluster on the busiest summit days. Before 2010, experienced climbers turned back on those days. Since 2010, almost no one does.
  • Earlier summit times account for nearly all of the decrease in descent deaths. Each hour later on the summit raises the odds of dying on the descent by about 40%.

Introduction

The 2012 study used data through spring 2010 and found that a veteran was as likely to die as a first-timer, and that commercial climbs were, if anything, safer. Since then, commercial climbing on the 8000 m peaks has gone from a large minority of climbs to the large majority. Most of that growth went to Nepali-owned operators, which typically cost much less than Western guiding companies. This update asks whether the original conclusions survive that change.

Methods

Data source

The Himalayan Database, originally derived from the archives of Elizabeth Hawley, records nearly every expedition to the Nepalese Himalaya since 1905. We used the version released in July 2026, which runs through the end of 2025.

Cohort and variables

We kept the original inclusion rules: climbs from 1970 onward, climbers who went above base camp, hired staff excluded, and one observation per climber per expedition. Each climber was linked across expeditions by name and year of birth, and "expedition number" counts that person's Himalayan expeditions in order, including those on peaks under 8000 m. The model was limited to the eight Nepalese 8000 m peaks, with each peak's subsidiary summits grouped with it (for example, Lhotse Shar with Lhotse). Covariates were the original set: peak, standard route, age, sex, season, summit success and calendar year.

Commercial status and operator

The current release has no commercial-expedition field. The database's COMRTE field marks commercial routes, not commercial expeditions. We therefore classified each expedition from its operator: a named guiding company in the sponsor field, or a Nepali full-service agency acting as the outfitter. Operators were grouped as Western, Nepali or other (mostly Chinese-run). Applied to 1970–2010, this label gives a 0.71% death rate on commercial climbs, against 0.7% in the original paper. A stricter version, counting only operators named in the sponsor field, is reported in the notes to each table.

Statistical methods

Multivariable logistic regression with generalised estimating equations, as in the original. Standard errors were clustered on both expedition and climber and combined (Miglioretti and Heagerty). Omnibus Wald tests are reported for peak, season and operator. Climbs with missing age (2.4%) were dropped. Cumulative career mortality was estimated by Kaplan–Meier, using expedition number as the time scale.

Summit-day decisions

The database records the date and outcome of each climber's summit bid, including bids that were turned back, and the reason the bid ended. We defined a "fatal day" as a peak and date on which anyone, hired staff included, died during a summit bid. To catch deaths on the descent, the day before a death also counted. Each client bid was flagged as on or off a fatal day. A logistic model of summit success included experience (log2 of expedition number), the fatal-day flag and their interaction, adjusted for peak, year, age, sex, commercial status and oxygen use, with standard errors clustered as above. A negative interaction means experienced climbers gave up more of their usual summit advantage on fatal days, which is to say they turned back.

Results

Descriptive analysis

The cohort grew from 39,260 climbs before summer 2010 to 65,398 by the end of 2025 (Table 1). In the newer period, climbers were older (mean age 42.8 vs 36.7) and more often women (19.0% vs 9.3%). Commercial expeditions rose from 22.5% of climbs to 77.6% across all peaks, and to 85.4% on the 8000 m peaks. The crude death rate halved, from 1.5% to 0.7%. Nepali-run expeditions became the largest group from about 2015 (fig 1).

Summit-day decisions

We identified 369 fatal days, and 5,835 client summit bids took place on them. These were the busiest summit days, not the stormiest: summit success was higher on fatal days than on other days (68% vs 57% before 2010, 84% vs 80% after). Deaths occurred in the main summit pushes.

Before 2010, summit success rose with experience on ordinary days, from 53% for first-timers to 65% for those on their 10th or later expedition (Table 6). On fatal days the gradient disappeared, at 66–69% in every band. On those days, turning back for weather, conditions or a late hour doubled from 7.8% of first-timers to 15.2% of the most experienced, and the most experienced died at about half the rate of everyone else (3.9% vs 7.4–8.8%). The interaction was significant (Table 7). After 2010 the turnaround pattern was gone. On fatal days, 79–86% of bidders summited in every band and only 4–6% turned back for weather or lateness. Among bidders who were on the mountain on a fatal day, more experience was still associated with lower odds of dying (0.75 per doubling, P=0.025), but the gradient was not consistent across bands.

Why fatality rates declined: summit-day volume, forecasting and timing

Volume and spread. On commercial expeditions in the spring and autumn seasons, the number of summiters per peak-season rose about sevenfold between the 1990s and 2015–19 (Table 8). Over the same period the turnaround rate decreased from 28% of bids to 6%. Summits did not, however, bunch into fewer days. Each season had about as many summit days as before, or more, and the share of a season's summits occurring on its three busiest days declined from 82% to 66%. Everest shows this most clearly. Between 2000–09 and 2020–25, summits per season more than tripled (181 to 619), the number of summit days rose from 8.4 to 12.4, and the share on the three busiest days decreased from 61% to 59%. Summit days became both bigger and more numerous.

Forecasting. Among commercial clients making a summit bid, the share of bids ended by bad weather decreased from 12.3% in 2000–09 to 2.7% in 2020–25, and summit success rose from 63% to 89% (Table 9). Deaths from falls decreased from 3.6 to 0.9 per 1,000 bids. Deaths from altitude illness and exhaustion did not decrease, and they now make up about two-thirds of summit-bid deaths. Until 2020, the three busiest days of each season were no safer than other days. In 2020–25 they were markedly safer, at 5.8 deaths per 1,000 bids against 10.0 on other days. Deaths did not cluster more over time: each fatal day averaged 1.0–1.5 commercial client deaths in every period.

Summit time and descent deaths. A summit time was recorded for 78% of client summiters, and for 84% of those who died on the descent. The later a climber summited, the more likely they were to die on the way down: about 2 deaths per 1,000 summiters for those on top before 8am, against 42 per 1,000 for those on top after 2pm (Table 10, fig 4). After adjustment for peak, year, age, sex, oxygen use and commercial status, each hour later on the summit raised the odds of dying on the descent by 38% (OR 1.38, 1.25 to 1.52, P<0.001). The figure for 2010–2025 alone was 47% (1.47, 1.35 to 1.60). Summit times have moved much earlier. On Everest, the median summit time went from 11:45 in the 1990s to about 7:00–7:30 after 2010, and the share of summits after noon dropped from 46% to 3–6%. Across the 8000 m peaks, half of summiters were on top before 8am in 2010–2025, against under a quarter before 2010.

This shift accounts for nearly all of the decrease in descent deaths. If we keep each time band's death rate fixed at its pooled value and change only the mix of summit times, the model predicts a decrease from 14.5 to 7.8 descent deaths per 1,000 summiters. The observed decrease was from 14.2 to 8.0. Within each time band, the risk did not improve: a summit after 2pm was as dangerous after 2010 (44 per 1,000) as before (41 per 1,000).

Discussion

Main findings

Experience. The original finding holds: overall, a client on their tenth Himalayan expedition is as likely to die as one on their first. Experience is not useless, though. Adjusted for oxygen use and peak, it raises the odds of summiting. The summit-day analysis also suggests why the survival benefit does not show up in the totals. Almost all the risk is concentrated on a few busy summit days. Before 2010, experienced climbers turned back more often on those days and died less often. Over a whole career that benefit was offset by riskier choices: going without oxygen, attempting harder peaks, and, for guides, staying high to help others. This is the trade-off the original authors suspected. Since 2010, the go/no-go decision has largely passed to the operator. Almost everyone on a fatal day now continues to the top, whatever their experience, so the turnaround benefit has gone. A smaller survival advantage for experienced climbers remains on those days.

Why deaths declined. The decline in deaths has three parts. First, up to 2019 most of it came from the shift to commercial expeditions, which went from 14% of 8000 m climbs in the 1990s to 89% in 2020–25. The commercial death rate itself stayed at 9–11 per 1,000 climbs until 2020. Second, there are clear signs of better forecasting. Bids ended by bad weather decreased by more than three-quarters, deaths from falls and exposure almost disappeared, and summits spread across more days each season even as volume rose sharply, rather than bunching into fewer days. Third, and most directly, climbers now reach the summit earlier. Each hour later on top raises the odds of dying on the descent by about 40%, and the move to early summits accounts for nearly all of the decrease in descent deaths per summiter. A late summit is as dangerous now as it ever was; fewer people make one. The data cannot separate forecasting from the other changes that make early summits possible, such as more oxygen, more Sherpa support and fixed ropes in place earlier. Nor can they rule out that slower, weaker climbers are both the ones who summit late and the ones who die. Deaths from altitude illness and exhaustion have not declined and are now the main remaining risk.

Commercialisation. The 2012 paper's "could in fact be safer" is now supported by significant results. Commercial status was associated with 39% lower odds of death over 1970–2025, and 56% lower over 2010–2025. The continuing calendar-year trend fits the original explanation: safety is improving through collective knowledge (logistics, forecasting, oxygen, rescue) rather than through any individual's experience.

Nepali versus Western operators. The change in who runs commercial expeditions has not made commercial climbing more dangerous than going without an operator: Nepali-run expeditions are still safer than non-commercial climbs. They are not as safe as Western-run expeditions, though. The odds of death were roughly twice as high overall and about 2.5 times as high on Everest. Oxygen use did not explain the gap. Western operators typically charge much more and provide more support, and that difference may be what this measures. The database does not record price, oxygen supply or Sherpa-to-client ratio, so the model cannot separate those effects. The gap narrowed in 2020–25.

Strengths and limitations

The near-complete coverage that strengthened the original study still applies, and the rebuild reproduced the published estimates. The main weakness is the commercial label. The 2012 authors took the database's own label at face value. That label is not in the current release, so we built one from operator names. It matches the original figures closely but not exactly, so results are shown for both a broad and a strict version. As in the original, clients and guides were not separated in the main model, hired staff are excluded, experience counts Nepalese expeditions only, and the data cover just the Nepalese 8000 m peaks. The summit-day analysis defines fatal days at the level of the whole peak rather than the route, depends on dated bids, and relies on turnaround reasons as recorded in the database.
