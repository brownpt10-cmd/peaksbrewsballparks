Does experience keep you alive in the Himalaya? What 55 years of data say

A non-technical companion to "Effects of experience and commercialisation on survival in Himalayan mountaineering: an update through 2025." Prepared October 2026 for Pat Brown.

The short version

  1. The mountains have become much safer. A climber's chance of dying on a given Himalayan expedition has roughly halved since 2010.
  2. Experience still doesn't lower your overall chance of dying. Climbers on their tenth expedition die at about the same rate as first-timers. That's true in the 2012 study's data and in the 15 years since.
  3. Climbing with a commercial operator lowers it. In 2012 this was only a hint. With the extra data it's a clear result: roughly 40–55% lower risk than climbing without an operator.
  4. The operator matters. Since 2010, clients of Western-run expeditions died at about 6 per 1,000 climbs and clients of Nepali-run expeditions at about 10 per 1,000. Climbers without an operator died at about 22 per 1,000.
  5. Experience shows up in one place: the decision to turn around. Before 2010, experienced climbers turned back more often on the days people died. Since 2010 almost no one turns back on those days, whatever their experience.
  6. The biggest single reason for fewer deaths is summiting earlier. Each hour later on the summit raises the risk of dying on the way down by about 40%. Half of summiters now reach the top before 8am, and that shift explains almost all of the drop in deaths on the descent.

The question

In 2012, three researchers asked two questions: do veteran Himalayan climbers survive better than beginners, and are guided commercial expeditions more or less dangerous than traditional ones? Using every recorded climb in Nepal from 1970 to spring 2010, they found that experience made no difference to survival, and that commercial climbs looked safer, though not by enough to rule out chance.

Since then the sport has changed a lot. Guided climbs went from about one climb in four to more than four in five, and most of that business moved from Western guiding companies to cheaper Nepali-owned operators. This report reruns their analysis on the full record through 2025 to see whether the answers changed.

The source is the Himalayan Database, which records nearly every expedition in Nepal. For each climber on each expedition it records age, sex, peak, route, whether they summited, whether they used oxygen, and whether and how they died. We linked each person's expeditions over the years, so we know whether a climb was their first, fifth or twentieth.

The main comparisons use the eight Nepalese peaks over 8,000 m (Everest, Cho Oyu, Lhotse, Makalu, Kangchenjunga, Dhaulagiri, Manaslu and Annapurna). That is where most climbing and most deaths happen.

Checking the method first. Before using anything new, we rebuilt the 2012 analysis on their 1970–2010 data. It reproduced their published numbers almost exactly. For example, they found Everest carried 0.42 times the odds of death of Annapurna, and the rebuild gives 0.42. So any change in the results comes from the new data, not a different method.

How to read the numbers

Death rate
Deaths divided by climbs. A rate of 1% means 1 death per 100 climbs.
"Adjusted" comparisons
Two groups can differ for reasons other than the one you care about. Commercial clients, for example, are more likely to be on Everest, which is safer than Annapurna. A regression model compares the groups as if they were otherwise alike, with the same peak, year, age, sex, season and route. The adjusted numbers here answer the question "all else equal, what difference does this factor make?"
Odds ratio (OR)
The model gives each factor as a multiplier on the odds of dying. An OR of 1.00 means no effect. An OR of 0.60 means 40% lower odds, and 2.0 means double. Death on a climb is rare, so odds and risk are almost the same thing, and you can read "0.60" as roughly "40% less likely to die."
95% confidence interval (the range in brackets)
The range of values consistent with the data. If the whole range sits on one side of 1.00, the effect is probably real. If it spans 1.00, the data can't tell the effect apart from no effect.
p-value
Roughly, how surprising the result would be if there were really no effect. Below 0.05 is the usual threshold for calling a result real. Above it means "could be chance."

1. The mountains have become much safer

Death rates have declined steadily for 50 years. After adjusting for everything else, each calendar year carries about 2% lower odds of death than the year before. Over 2010–2025 the crude death rate across all peaks was 0.7%, half the 1.5% of 1970–2010.

The figure below shows what that means over a career. Picture someone who keeps going back to the Himalaya, one expedition after another. Under 1970–2010 conditions, about 1 in 10 such climbers would have died by their seventh expedition and 1 in 5 by their fifteenth. Under 2010–2025 conditions those figures are about 1 in 18 and 1 in 9.

The 2012 authors credited this improvement to collective knowledge rather than to any individual: better weather forecasts, fixed ropes, oxygen systems, rescue and logistics. That explanation still fits the data.

2. Experience still doesn't lower your overall chance of dying

This is the result that surprises people, and it held up again.

After adjusting for peak, year and the rest, each additional expedition changes the odds of dying by a factor of 0.99. That is essentially 1.00, or no effect, and the range of 0.96 to 1.01 excludes any meaningful benefit. For 2010–2025 alone, the model first suggested a small benefit (0.95 per expedition). That turned out to come from a small group of very experienced expedition leaders and professional guides. With them set aside, the effect disappears.

Experience does help with one thing: getting to the top. Experienced climbers use supplemental oxygen less often (73% vs 85% of first-timers after 2010) and choose different peaks. Comparing like with like, each doubling of a climber's experience (say from 2 expeditions to 4) raises the odds of summiting by about 13% after 2010 and about 33% before. So experience gets you up the mountain more often. It just doesn't make you less likely to die there. Section 4 suggests why.

3. Commercial expeditions are safer, and the operator matters

Over the whole 1970–2025 record, commercial climbers had about 40% lower odds of dying than climbers without an operator: OR 0.61, range 0.44 to 0.84. The whole range sits below 1.00, so this is very unlikely to be chance. Looking at 2010–2025 alone, the advantage was larger, at about 56% lower odds (OR 0.44). In 2012 the same comparison gave 0.63 with a range that crossed 1.00, so the original authors could only call it a possibility. With 15 more years of data it is a firm result.

Compared directly, Nepali-run clients had roughly 1.8 to 2 times the odds of dying of Western-run clients. Across all eight peaks that estimate isn't quite precise enough to be certain: depending on exactly how commercial expeditions are defined, the range either just includes or just excludes 1.00. On Everest it is clear, at about 2.4 times (range 1.2 to 4.9). The difference is not explained by oxygen use. The database doesn't record price, oxygen supply per client or the number of Sherpas per client, so we can't say which of those is responsible. Western operators typically charge much more and provide more support on all of these.

Two points keep this in proportion. First, Nepali-run expeditions are still about twice as safe as climbing without an operator, so the shift toward them has not made commercial climbing more dangerous. Second, the gap is closing. In 2020–25 the crude rates were about 6 deaths per 1,000 climbs for Nepali-run clients and 4 per 1,000 for Western-run clients.

4. The summit-day decision

If experienced climbers get up the mountain more often, why don't they survive better? To find out, we looked at individual summit days. The database records the date of every summit attempt, including attempts that turned back, and why each one ended. We marked every "fatal day": a day on which someone, Sherpa staff included, died during a summit attempt on that peak. There were 369 such days. We then compared what climbers did on those days with what they did on all other days.

Fatal days are not stormy days. They are the busiest days, when a good weather window opens and everyone goes for the top. Summit success was actually higher on fatal days (68% vs 57% before 2010). Deaths happen during the big summit pushes, often on the way down.

Before 2010, experience showed up as judgement. On normal days, veterans summited more often than beginners. On fatal days they gave up that advantage: they were about twice as likely as first-timers to turn back, and the most experienced died at less than half the rate of everyone else. The statistical test of this pattern is solid. The chance it is a fluke is under 1%, and it is stronger still when expedition leaders and guides are left out.

Since 2010, that pattern is gone. On fatal days, 79–86% of climbers in every experience group went to the top, and only about 4–6% turned back for weather or lateness. Among climbers caught out on a fatal day, more experience was still linked to somewhat better survival. That link is weaker and less consistent: the 2nd–4th-expedition group fared worst, not the first-timers.

5. Why the mountains got safer: forecasting and the clock

More people, spread over more days. You might expect bigger crowds to pack into fewer days. On commercial expeditions the opposite happened. On Everest, summiters per season went from about 180 in the 2000s to about 620 in 2020–25. Over the same period the number of summit days per season rose from about 8 to 12, and the busiest three days carried a slightly smaller share of the total. Each summit day is bigger than it used to be, and there are more of them.

The forecasting signature. Commercial clients rarely get turned back by weather now: it happens to 3% of summit attempts, down from 12%. That is the main reason summit success went from about 63% to 89%. Deaths from falls decreased by about three-quarters, and deaths from exposure became rare. Deaths from altitude illness and exhaustion, on the other hand, haven't decreased at all, and they now make up about two-thirds of summit-day deaths. A forecast can tell you when the wind will drop, but it doesn't change what 8,000 m does to the body.

The clock matters most. The database records when each person reached the summit. The later the summit, the more likely the climber died on the way down:

Comparing like with like, each extra hour on the way to the top raises the odds of dying on the descent by about 40%. Summiting at 2pm instead of 8am multiplies the risk roughly sevenfold. Summit times have moved much earlier: the median Everest summit time was about 11:45am in the 1990s and is about 7–7:30am now.

A simple calculation shows how much this matters. Keep the death rate for each time slot fixed, change only how many people summit in each slot, and the predicted descent death rate decreases from 14.5 to 7.8 per 1,000 summiters. The actual decrease was from 14.2 to 8.0. The move to earlier summits accounts for almost the whole improvement. Summiting late is just as dangerous as it ever was (about 4 in 100 after 2pm, then and now). Far fewer people do it.

What this can't prove. Earlier summits come from several changes at once: better forecasts, more oxygen, more Sherpa support, and fixed ropes in place sooner. The data can't separate them. It's also possible that some of the link runs the other way: slow, struggling climbers are both the ones who summit late and the ones who would have been at risk anyway. Even so, nothing else in the data comes close to explaining as much of the improvement.

Before 2020, most of the drop in the overall death rate came from more climbers joining commercial expeditions (14% of 8,000 m climbs in the 1990s, 89% now). The death rate on commercial expeditions themselves stayed at about 9–11 per 1,000 climbs until 2020, then decreased to about 6. That recent drop matches the shift to early summits.

Putting it together

The data suggest two forces that cancel out, which is why "experience doesn't help" appears in the totals:

  • Experience helps on the decisive day. Nearly all the risk is concentrated on a handful of busy summit days, and experienced climbers have tended to read those days better.
  • Experience also leads people into more risk. Veterans more often climb without oxygen, attempt harder peaks, and, if they are guides, stay high to help others. Over a career those choices use up the advantage.

The 2012 authors suggested this trade-off but couldn't show it. The summit-day data are consistent with it. There is also a newer twist. On a modern commercial expedition the operator usually makes the go/no-go decision for everyone, so an individual's judgement matters less than it used to, and the operator's judgement matters more. That fits the finding that the choice of operator now makes a bigger difference to survival than the climber's experience.

What could change these conclusions

  • The "commercial" label had to be rebuilt. The current database has no field marking commercial expeditions, so we classified them from operator names. That label matches the 2012 study's figures closely but not perfectly, and the main results were checked with two versions of it.
  • Sherpa and other hired staff are excluded, as in the original study. Their risks are different and mostly occur while preparing the route.
  • Experience only counts Nepal. A climber with 20 Alaska or Andes trips but a first Himalayan expedition counts as a first-timer.
  • Some things that matter aren't recorded, including price, oxygen supply per client, Sherpa-to-client ratio and fitness.
  • The summit-day analysis counts a fatal day across a whole peak, not a single route, and leaves out the 3–14% of turned-back attempts that have no recorded date.
