Ama Dablam on the Summit Clock

The Everest study asked how late is too late, and how many people a rope can take. This brief runs the same questions on Ama Dablam’s Southwest Ridge (6,812 m), ahead of Paul Clarke and Xenia’s 2026 attempt. They plan to go without Sherpas, cache summit gear high and summit at sunrise on 28 October.

The short version
  1. Be on top by about 10:30. The slowest quarter of climbers take 6 hours or more to get back to Camp 2, and the sun sets about 17:27 in late October. Teams already do this: the median summit time has been 08:30–09:00 every year since 2021, down from about noon in the 2000s.
  2. Leave Camp 2 around midnight to 02:00. Since 2015 the median Camp 2 departure is 01:00. The push takes about 7.5–8.5 hours, and the way back down to Camp 2 about 4.5.
  3. Through 2025 the rope was never full. The busiest day had 47 people on the summit push and the busiest hour 19 summits. Busy days were earlier, not later. 2026 is past that record: more than 200 climbers were reported heading up over the weekend of 24–25 October.
  4. The simulator says the queue is not the problem up to about 100 a day. At an assumed 24 people an hour through the Mushroom Ridge, waits stay under 10 minutes at 100 a day. They reach about 30 minutes at 150 and 1.5 hours at 200. The bigger lever is when a team leaves.
  5. On Ama Dablam the clock is the smaller risk. Of 30 deaths since 1990, 10 were falls and 10 were serac, avalanche, rock or ice. The worst was a serac onto Camp 3 in 2006 that killed six. The first death of 2026 was a fall from the Camp 3 area on 26 October, the morning after a summit.

1. The route

The normal route is the Southwest Ridge, climbed mostly in autumn. Climbers go from Base Camp (about 4,600 m) to Camp 1 (5,700 m), then up the Yellow Tower to a small, exposed Camp 2 (about 6,000 m; the ExplorersWeb guide says room for 7–8 tents). The summit push goes past the Grey Tower and across the knife-edged Mushroom Ridge, which the ExplorersWeb guide calls the crux of summit day (about 40 minutes). It continues to Camp 3 (about 6,300 m), then up the face beside the hanging glacier, the Dablam, to the top. Many teams skip Camp 3 because the Dablam hangs above it. The whole route above Camp 1 is fixed rope.3,4

2. When people reach the top

There were 2,590 timed summits from 2010 to 2025. The middle half reached the top between 08:00 and 11:45 (median 09:45), and only 27 before first light. The big change is the trend: until about 2015 the median was close to noon. Since 2017 it has been 08:30–09:30 every year except 2020, when there were few climbers.

3. Leaving camp

Departure times are pulled from the expedition route notes and were checked by hand on Everest, where about 97% were right. For Camp 2 there are 77 usable times since 2010. The middle half left between 00:30 and 03:30, with a few as early as 22:00 and some as late as 05:00–06:00. The push from Camp 2 has a median of about 8.5 hours since 2010. Climbers who started from Camp 3 needed about 5 hours to the top. Coming down, the median summit-to-Camp-2 time is 4.5 hours and the slowest quarter take 6 hours or more. Summit to Camp 3 takes about 3 hours.

The daylight rule. Sunset is 17:27 in late October and 17:18 by mid-November. Take away 6 hours for a slow descent to Camp 2 and an hour of margin, and you need to be on top by about 10:15–10:30. A 01:00 start and an average 7.5–8.5 hour push gets you there between 08:30 and 09:30, with an hour or more to spare.

4. Crowds

Ama Dablam is busy. It has had 470–520 summits a season since 2022, with 48–65 summit days a season and 6–9 days with 20 or more summits. But in the Himalayan Database (to 2025) it has never been busy enough to slow down. The most people on the summit push in one day was 47, on 9 November 2024 and again on 27 October 2025. The most summits in any one hour was 15–19. On days with more people, the median summit was earlier. Allowing for the year, each 10 extra people meant about 30 minutes earlier (p = 0.002). Busy days are good-weather days with organized teams that leave early. The push itself did not get longer on busy days (−0.2 h per 10 people, p = 0.41).

2026 is different. Rope fixers reached the top at 11:40 on 13 October. The Department of Tourism had issued 178 foreign permits by then, and more than 300 had applied by 17 October. ExplorersWeb reported more than 200 climbers lined up toward the summit over the weekend of 24–25 October, and over 200 summits so far this season. That is roughly twice the busiest day in the record, so the record can’t say how the rope behaves. That is the simulator’s job. Operators now fix double ropes on much of the route so people can go up and down at the same time.5,6,7

5. What the simulator shows

The model works like the Everest one. People leave Camp 2 at recorded departure times and climb at their own pace. They pass the Mushroom Ridge one at a time in each direction, top out, and come back down. The rope speed has never been measured, because the mountain was never full. So it is a setting: 17, 24 or 40 people an hour, all faster than the 19-an-hour peak actually seen. These runs use separate up and down ropes, 1.69 people on the rope per client (the historical share on busy days) and 20 runs each.

Free-for-all departures drawn from the 77 recorded Camp 2 times, rope speed 24 an hour. At 17 an hour the 150-person wait grows to about 2 hours. At 40 an hour even 200 people wait under 10 minutes.

Three things stand out:

  • Up to about 100 a day, the late summits come from late starts, not queues. About 4 in 10 recorded Camp 2 departures are 03:00 or later, and those climbers summit after 10:30 even on an empty mountain.
  • Past about 150 a day, the ridge becomes the clock. Waits grow fast, and the risk per person starts to rise.
  • Booked start times set the real capacity. With slots every 2.6 minutes from 23:30 and everyone on top by 10:30, the ridge takes about 77 people a day at 24 an hour, 55 at 17 an hour and about 130 at 40 an hour. Starting at 22:00 adds about 35 more at the middle speed. The rule is the same as on Everest: people per day ≈ rope speed × the hours of departures that still top out by 10:30.

6. Risk and the clock

On Ama Dablam 11 people died on the way down after a summit bid from 1990 to 2025, out of about 8,100 bidders. That is 1.35 per 1,000, a third of Everest’s rate. There are too few deaths to fit a time slope from Ama Dablam alone. So the model borrows the 8,000 m slope (each hour later multiplies the odds by 1.37) and sets the level to Ama Dablam’s own record. For a client that gives about 1.4 per 1,000 for a 10:00 summit, 2.7 at noon and 5.0 at 14:00. Hired staff are about a quarter of that. Only one recorded summit bid was turned back (1 of 414), so a turnaround is almost never used on this mountain.

The main danger is falling and things falling. A crowd adds to both: more people under the Dablam and on loose ground means more people in the way of whatever comes down. The ExplorersWeb guide warns about rockfall with crowds in particular. 2026’s first death fits the falls pattern. A 57-year-old German climber summited on 25 October with his guide, stopped high and spent the night at Camp 3. At about 08:00 the next morning he was found missing from where he’d been asked to wait, without his crampons on, and was later found about 1,700 m below.3,8,9

8. Paul Clarke and Xenia: the plan against the record

Paul set out the plan in a live stream from Base Camp on 26 October (“Can We Do It? Our Solo Strategy for Ama Dablam”).11 He summited Ama Dablam in October 2025, which matches the Himalayan Database record of 27 October 2025: the record day, with Himalayan Guides. This time it’s the two of them with no personal Sherpa support. Xenia is aiming to be the first Hungarian woman on top.

Why it’s a good plan against the clock and the crowd. A sunrise summit is about as far inside the daylight rule as anyone gets. The modeled risk of dying on the way down is about 0.4 per 1,000, a seventh of the noon value. It also puts them ahead of the wave: the main group leaves Camp 2 from midnight to 02:00, so a 23:45 start crosses the Mushroom Ridge before the queue forms. In the simulator, with about 100 on the push and a 6-hour climb, they reach the top around 05:45 with only a handful ahead of them.

What it trades.

  • They come down through everyone going up. On a day of about 100, the simulator has them passing more than 90 climbers on the ropes between the top and Camp 2, from about 06:00 to 09:00. Double ropes help, but every pass on an anchor or a rappel takes time and care. Falls on the descent are the biggest single cause of death in the record.
  • They climb the coldest hours in the dark, without Sherpas to share loads or the work if something goes wrong.
  • They’re on top before the sun reaches the route. Rockfall from crowds is a known hazard, but on the way up nobody is above them. On the way down the risk is mostly theirs to cause, onto the people below.
  • Exposure to the hanging glacier. If they stay at Camp 2 rather than Camp 3, they keep the night out from under the Dablam.
Still unknown: which camp they leave from, the exact departure time and how big the wave is on 28 October. Methods and limits

Data: Himalayan Database, Ama Dablam (AMAD) 1990–2025. That is 12,304 climbers (2,836 hired), 7,057 summits and 3,996 of them timed. A bidder is anyone with a recorded summit bid. A day’s load is the number of bidders on that summit date. Summit times are Nepal time and self-reported. Departure times come from expedition route notes using the method of The Summit Clock (am/pm resolved against the summit time). The risk model uses the 8,000 m logistic slope from The Summit Clock with an intercept fitted to the Ama Dablam descent-death rate. With 11 such deaths, Ama Dablam alone can’t confirm the slope. Light times use astral at the summit position for 27 October and 10 November. The simulator leaves out weather, rockfall, rescues and clients who are slower than staff. Ridge speed is an assumption. 2026 figures come from news reports and will change.
