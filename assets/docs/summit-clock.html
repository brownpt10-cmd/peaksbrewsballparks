Mountains · Summit timing & route capacity

The Summit Clock

When climbers reach the top of Nepal’s eight-thousanders, how that hour relates to dying on the way down, and how many people one fixed rope can move before the clock runs out.

1Question and data

This study asks three linked questions. When should a climber be on top of each 8,000 m peak in Nepal, and when do teams leave high camp to get there? Does the number of people on the route on summit day push summit times later? And how many people can a route carry before that crowding costs lives?

The data are the Himalayan Database, the expedition archive begun by Elizabeth Hawley and maintained by Richard Salisbury and colleagues, in the Autumn–Winter 2025 release (Himal 2.75). The study uses the eight main summits recorded there: Everest, Kangchenjunga, Lhotse, Makalu, Cho Oyu, Dhaulagiri I, Manaslu and Annapurna I, for 1990 through 2025. Everest is split into the South Col and North Ridge routes because they are separate ropes. Subsidiary summits such as Yalung Kang and Lhotse Shar are excluded.

2Late summits and descent deaths

The descent-death rate rises steadily with the hour of the summit. No hour works as a clean cutoff; risk climbs from the first light onward and steepens through late morning.

A logistic model separates the hour from other differences between climbers. It adjusts for route, year, oxygen use, age, sex and hired status, with standard errors clustered by expedition (n = 18,992 timed summits with known age; 128 descent deaths).

The hourly effect holds in every cut tried: members only (OR 1.39), 1990–2009 (1.37), 2010–2025 (1.33), Everest only (1.39), and with time measured as hours after local sunrise instead of clock time (1.37). Route-specific slopes do not differ from one another (Wald test p = 0.35). The routes differ in their baseline risk, but the penalty for each extra hour is about the same on all of them.

The pattern matches the earlier Everest study by Firth and colleagues, which found median summit times of 09:00–09:59 for survivors and 13:00–13:59 for those who died on the way down (Firth et al., 2008). In this larger multi-peak set the medians are 08:30 and 12:40.

Cause or symptom?

A late summit is partly a marker of a climber who is already in trouble: slow, hypoxic, cold or ill. Firth and colleagues describe fatigue and late summit times as early signs of the condition that later kills. The 1.37 figure measures how risk tracks the clock. It does not prove that the clock alone causes the deaths. That is why a turnaround time works: it catches the climber whose pace is already a warning.

3The summit day, route by route

The database has no field for when a team left high camp. Hawley’s route narratives often record it, though, in sentences such as “left C4 at 9:00 pm on 20 May”. A text parser pulled departure times from the expedition notes, kept only departures from the expedition’s highest camp on the summit push, converted Chinese time where the note says so, and resolved am/pm against the recorded summit time. Two hand audits of 30 random extractions found 26 and then, after fixes, 29 correct. Return times to high camp were extracted the same way for 500 expeditions.

A “be on top by” time for each route

The recommended latest summit time is the earlier of two limits.

Daylight. Sunset at high camp on the route’s typical summit date, minus the 75th-percentile descent time from summit to high camp, minus one hour of margin.

Risk. The hour at which the model’s descent-death risk reaches 10 per 1,000 for a reference client: a 40-year-old male member using oxygen, in 2025.

Both limits are choices. The table shows the inputs so a reader can apply a stricter or looser standard.

Annapurna I has only six recorded descent times, so its daylight limit uses the pooled 75th percentile (6.9 h). Kangchenjunga (14) and Dhaulagiri (13) are also thin. Kangchenjunga’s overall median summit time is 09:00 (members 09:55; members since 2010, 08:00), but it was 14:00–14:15 through the 1990s and 2000s. The eight Kangchenjunga climbers who died descending had a median summit time of 16:22.

An earlier departure predicts an earlier summit on eight of nine routes (Spearman ρ 0.37–0.74, all p < 0.02; Dhaulagiri ρ 0.14, p = 0.45). Departure is the one lever a team fully controls.

4Does a crowded day push the summit later?

The simple comparison says no. On Everest’s South Col route, the busiest days have summit times as early as or earlier than quiet days. The quietest days (1–10 people) are the latest of all: small independent teams, climbers without oxygen, and pushes in poor windows. Crowded days are the best weather windows with full commercial support. That is selection, and it hides any crowding effect.

Comparing members of the same expedition who went up on different days removes most of that selection. Even then, more people on the rope does not move the summit clock (South Col: −1 minute per 100 extra people, 95% CI −29 to +26). The departure times show why.

Daily crowd size shows no detectable link to deaths on summit day. That agrees with Huey and colleagues, who found no crowding effect on success or death on Everest in 2018–2019 and suggested that waiting at high camp for good weather may mask it (Huey et al., 2020). Their count of 396 summit bids on 23 May 2019, across both sides, matches this reconstruction: 241 on the South Col and 158 on the North Ridge, 399 in total.

The finding is not that crowds are harmless. Through 2025, operators have kept the summit clock steady by starting earlier, and the busiest days have stayed just below the point where that stops working. The next section finds that point.

5How many people can one rope move?

The summit ridge on the South Col route is one fixed line. Climbers clip past one another at anchors, and at the step below the summit only one person moves at a time. Going up and coming down share that line. It behaves like a single-lane bridge with a light that changes direction.

The trafficability rule

The number of people a route can put on top before the turnaround time is set by its slowest section:

Nsafe ≈ μ × W

μ is the throughput of the bottleneck in people per hour, with two-way traffic. W is the usable summit window: the earliest practical summit time to the turnaround time. On the South Col route, μ ≈ 29 per hour, measured on the busiest well-recorded days, and W ≈ 03:30 to 11:00, or 7.5 hours. That gives Nsafe ≈ 29 × 7.5 ≈ 220 people on the rope. This is the same relation as Little’s law for queues: people in the system equal arrival rate times time spent.

Peak-hour counts are scaled up for summiters with no recorded time. Summit times cluster on :00 and :30 (53% of records), which inflates single-hour peaks. The sustained rate, 80% of summiters divided by the 10th-to-90th percentile window, is the more reliable measure. Days with under 75% of times recorded are least reliable.

Simulation

To see what happens as N grows, a simulation sends N people up the route one at a time. Departure times are drawn from the South Col departures recorded since 2010. Each person climbs to the foot of the summit section at their own pace, then passes through a single-lane section that takes a fixed number of minutes per person. Changing direction from up to down costs extra minutes for clipping and passing. Summiters spend 20 minutes on top and then must pass back through the same section going down.

The three unknowns were fitted to the two best-recorded big days in 2019 and to 19 May 2016. The model has to match their summit-time spread, the queue cost seen in this data (busy-day pushes run about 30 minutes longer), and the waits of up to 2–3 hours reported at the Hillary Step (Jenkins, 2013; Arnette, 2019).

6Turnaround, departure slots and a daily cap

The turnaround climbers actually use

“Be on top by 1 pm” is the rule most often quoted. On the South Col route since 2010, clients beat it comfortably: 90% summit by 11:00 and 95% by 11:40 (n = 2,409). The de facto turnaround for most teams is 11:30 to noon. A tail ignores any rule: 3.4% of clients summit after 13:00 and 1% after 14:37. In 1990–2009, 9.6% of clients summited after 13:00.

That tail carries a disproportionate share of the deaths. Since 2010, the 6.5% of South Col summits made after noon account for 6 of the 23 timed descent deaths (26%). Summits before 11:00 carried 2.7 deaths per 1,000; summits after 13:00 carried 19 per 1,000. For the model’s reference client the risk is about 10 per 1,000 at 11:00 and about 18 per 1,000 at 13:00, two and a half and almost five times the 08:00 level.

Turning back is cheap by comparison. Across all eight peaks, climbers who turned back because of weather, conditions or the clock died on the descent at 4.6 per 1,000 (15 of 3,254). On the South Col none of 617 did.

2026: a summit at 17:30

Arun Kumar Tiwari reached the top on 21 May at about 17:30, according to Rishi Bhandari of the Expedition Operators Association (PTI, 2026). That is almost seven hours past the South Col be-on-top-by time in §3. He died on the descent near the Hillary Step (Gripped, 2026). No turnaround rule was applied.

Enforcing the turnaround in the simulation

The simulation from §5 was extended in three ways. First, 44% of the people on the rope are clients and 56% are Sherpas or guides, each with their own risk from the model in §2 (hired staff have about a quarter of the odds). Second, a turnaround is enforced: anyone who cannot start up the summit section in time to top out by the turnaround turns back, and the line is freed for others. Turned-back climbers carry the 4.6 per 1,000 descent-death rate above. Third, departures can be booked: instead of today’s pattern, the N people leave in evenly spaced slots between two times. Departures earlier than 19:00 were not allowed, since only 5% of recorded South Col departures leave before then.

A day is acceptable when 95% of those who start up summit before the turnaround and the 90th-percentile wait at the summit section stays under 60 minutes (about a quarter bottle of oxygen at 3 L/min) or, for a stricter standard, 30 minutes. The model gives no penalty for night hours or cold beyond that wait limit, so it cannot reward sending everyone out at dusk.

A daily cap

The table gives the most people the South Col rope can carry in one summit push under each rule. Each range runs from the congested calibration to the base calibration.

On today’s single two-way rope the answer is 150 to 175 people a day, or 66 to 77 clients, with booked departures between 19:00 and 23:00 (one person about every two minutes, or a client-and-Sherpa pair about every four) and a turnaround of 11:00 to 12:00. At that load the model gives 1.7–2.0 descent deaths per 1,000 who start up, about half of the 3.0–3.6 it gives for the same numbers with today’s departures and no turnaround. On the stricter 30-minute standard the cap is 125–150 (55–66 clients). With separate up and down lines it is 375 to more than 450.

A 13:00 turnaround with today’s departures allows 175–200 a day but turns around 4–5% of climbers and leaves 1.9–2.2 deaths per 1,000. Moving the turnaround earlier and booking departures is what makes the day safer. Neither needs new equipment.

From a daily cap to permits

A daily cap turns the summit push into a fixed number of seats per weather window instead of a rush on the first good day. The number of usable days sets how many seats a season holds. Counting days with at least 20 summits on the South Col, the 13 normal seasons from 2010 to 2025 (2014, 2015 and 2020 excluded) had 4 to 9, with a median of 7.

Nepal issued 478 Everest permits in 2023 and 495 in 2026 (AFP, 2024; Arnette, 2026). That fits a typical season on one rope but not a poor one. The bigger problem is concentration: in an average season since 2010 the three busiest days carried about two-thirds of all summits, and in 2026 about 80% of summits came in ten days (Arnette, 2026).

A workable scheme has three parts. Permits are set to the cap times a poor-to-typical season, about 330–540 clients on one rope. Each permit carries a summit-day slot and a departure slot, assigned by lottery or by booking once the weather windows are forecast. When a window closes early, the unused slots roll to the next window in order. Operators can then plan oxygen, Sherpa staffing and rope work against a known number instead of a guess about everyone else.

7What this means

For a climber. Treat the be-on-top-by time in §3 as a hard turnaround and work back from it. On the South Col that is 10:45, not 13:00. Each hour of slack spent in a queue cuts the margin as much as an hour of slow climbing. On a forecast big day, leave earlier rather than hope the line moves.

For operators. Capacity is μ × W. Leaving earlier widens W, but only until climbers reach the summit section in full dark and cold. Booked departure slots spread the arrivals and remove the late starters who make up most of the after-noon tail. Alternating up and down traffic in blocks, rather than one at a time, cuts the time lost to passing. A second, one-way line on the summit section raises μ more than anything else modeled. Operators proposed this for the Hillary Step in 2014 (Sharma, 2014).

For regulators. Nepal’s Supreme Court in 2024 told the government to set Everest permits by carrying capacity (AFP, 2024). Through May 2026 no numeric limit was in law (Prasain, 2026). Measured on the rope, carrying capacity is a daily number, not a seasonal one. On one rope it is about 150–175 people, or 66–77 clients, per summit push. A seasonal permit cap only helps if each permit also carries a summit day.

8Spring 2026, provisional

The 2026 season is not yet in the Himalayan Database. What is reported so far:

  • Summits. 1,008 from Nepal, a record, per the Department of Tourism (Kunwar, 2026). The Tibet side was closed to foreign teams, so the season’s crowd was on the South Col rope (The New Daily, 2026). About 80% of summits came between 17 and 26 May (Arnette, 2026).
  • Record day. 20 May: 274 summits according to the Expedition Operators Association, 270 in Arnette’s count. At about 90% of people on the rope reaching the top on big days, that is roughly 300 on the rope and 110–120 clients, near twice the cap in §6.
  • Permits. 495 Everest permits; Arnette’s rough split is 40% clients and 60% support (Arnette, 2026).
  • Summit-day deaths. Sandeep Are summited on 20 May, developed snow blindness, was brought down from the South Summit by five Sherpas and died at Camp 2 (Gripped, 2026). Arun Kumar Tiwari summited on 21 May at about 17:30 and died near the Hillary Step on the descent (PTI, 2026; Gripped, 2026). Both climbed with Pioneer Adventures. Reports differ on causes (pulmonary oedema for Tiwari in Gripped; exhaustion in Outlook Traveller; cerebral oedema for Are in Outlook Traveller) and on dates (Outlook Traveller puts both summits on 21 May).
  • Rope speed, estimated. About 300 people on the rope and reports of about 12 hours from the South Col to the top (against a usual 10.5) point to a median wait of about an hour and a half. The model matches that when each person takes about a minute on the single-file summit section, a sustained rate of about 25 people an hour both ways (plausible range 21–28), a little slower than 2019’s 29. At that rate the middle 80% of the day’s summits would have taken about 9 hours, roughly 03:30 to 12:30. This is an engineering estimate, not a measurement; the database update will allow a direct check.
  • Not yet available. Individual summit times, departure times, daily counts other than 20 May and measured queue lengths. The study will be rerun when the Spring 2026 database update is released.

9Limits of this study

  • Missing times. A quarter of summits have no recorded time, and the gaps are not random (2024: 68 of 210 timed on the busiest day). Times cluster on the hour and half-hour.
  • Few deaths. 128 events in the main model. Route-level risk estimates have wide intervals, especially for Annapurna, Dhaulagiri and Kangchenjunga.
  • Lateness as symptom. As noted in §2, summit time partly reflects the climber’s condition. The 1.37 odds ratio is an association.
  • Text extraction. Departures come from narrative notes, about 97% accurate on audit. Expeditions whose notes record a departure may differ from those whose notes do not.
  • People on the rope counts bidders recorded with a bid date. About 6% of non-summit bidders have no date. Lhotse climbers share the South Col route up to Camp 3 but are counted separately.
  • Simulation. One bottleneck, no weather and no rescues. Its early tail runs about an hour earlier than observed. Turned-back climbers are given the all-route turnback death rate; night hours and cold count only through the wait limit. Use it to compare rules, not to forecast a given day.
  • Usable days are counted from days with 20 or more summits, which reflects demand as well as weather.
  • Coverage. The database ends with the 2025 seasons. Spring 2026, including the 20 May 2026 record day, is not yet in it.

10Reproducing the study

The scripts and output tables are kept with the study’s working files and are available on request. Run them in order: load.py → cohort.py → timing.py → risk.py → dep2.py → depsum.py → desc.py → ideal.py → crowd.py–crowd4.py → thru.py → calib.py → scen.py → scen2.py → sens.py → turn.py → opt.py (uses sim2.py) → optsum.py → profile.py → campdep.py → campdeath.py → causes.py → bundle.py → bundle2.py. Python 3 with pandas, statsmodels, dbfread and astral. Sunrise and sunset are computed for each summit’s coordinates and elevation.
