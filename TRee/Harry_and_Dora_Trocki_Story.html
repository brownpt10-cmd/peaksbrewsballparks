A family history

Harry & Dora

From Swansea to Chicago, 1914–1998

1881–1922The Trocki Story

The Trocki story begins in the Suwałki region, a corner of lakes, forests and small farms in the far northeast of what is now Poland, near the borders of Lithuania and East Prussia. The province was a patchwork: Lithuanian villages to the north, Jewish merchants and Russian officials and soldiers in the towns, and in the south, around Augustów, Polish-speaking Catholic farm families whose lives centered on the parish church. That southern, Polish corner was John and Bernice’s world: Bernice was baptized in the parish of Bargłów, and the Trocki name fills the parish books of Augustów, Wigry and Suwałki. When John and Bernice were born it was part of the Russian Empire, which is why the 1910 census lists them as born in “Russia.” Like millions of other Polish peasants of that time, they left za chlebem, “for bread,” and like so many of them they went to Chicago, the capital of Polish America.

Bronisława Dudek, known in Chicago as Bernice (and sometimes “Bessie” or “Bronia”), was baptized in 1883 in the parish of Bargłów Kościelny, in the Augustów district. She was born in the village of Dręstwo (Dreństwo in the register). Her parents were Kazimierz Dudek and Karolina Formejster, the “Casimir Dudkowski and Caroline Farmeister” named on her Chicago death record. She was one of at least seven children: Józefa (1881), Bronisława (1883), Marianna (1887), Józef (1890), Marcel (1893), Kazimierz (1896) and Jan Bolesław (1899). By 1890 the family had moved a few miles north into the parish of Rajgród, a small market town on Lake Rajgrodzkie.

On 5 May 1901, aged 17, “Bronisława Dutkowska” of Rajgród stepped off the Hamburg-America liner SS Batavia at Ellis Island. She had sailed from Hamburg, alone, and gave her destination as a cousin, Jan Cihovsky, in Cleveland. Within three years she was in Chicago.

Jan (John Alex) Trocki gave his birth date in America as 12 December 1881, and his birthplace as “Sowalski,” meaning the Suwałki province. Trocki was a well-established name in the region; the parish books of Augustów, Wigry and Suwałki record dozens of Trocki baptisms in the 1870s and 1880s. One stands out. In 1880 a Jan Trocki, son of Michał Trocki and Wiktoria Borzewicz, was baptized in Bargłów Kościelny, Bernice’s own parish, from the village of Netta. If he is our John, he and Bernice grew up a few miles apart in the same parish, which would explain how two young people from the same corner of Poland found each other in Chicago. (Two other Jan Trockis were baptized in Wigry in 1882, so this still needs confirming.)

John followed Bernice to America in 1903. On 7 June 1904, Jan Trocki and Bronisława Dudkowska were married in Chicago.

Was the name changed? In the records found so far, the surname Trocki stays the same: Trocki was a common name in the Suwałki and Augustów parishes, and John appears as Trocki in Chicago from 1904 onward. Bernice’s surname did change. She was baptized Dudek; her brother Marcel was entered as “Dudkowski” in 1893, she arrived in 1901 as “Dutkowska,” and in America she was Dudkowska. Their first names changed too: Jan became John, and Bronisława became Bernice. An earlier family name for John, if there was one, may still turn up in his naturalization papers or the 1904 church marriage record.

Their children were baptized with Polish names that turned into American ones as they grew up. Mieczysław became Michel or Mitchell (born 1907), then came John (1909), Wacław became Walter (baptized June 1910), Kazimierz became Casimir (born 1 November 1914), Eugenia became Jennie (1917), and there was Genevieve. The last was Harry, born 15 August 1922. Birth and baptism records also name a Bronisław and a Herman, who may have died young or gone by other names.

By 1930 the family owned their own home at 2147 Dickens Avenue, in the Polish neighborhoods of Chicago’s Northwest Side, valued at $13,500. John was a tailor. Both he and Bernice had become American citizens. Polish was the language at home, and the census taker recorded that Bernice could not read or write.

A name puzzle, solved: On the original 1930 census page the youngest son is written as Roman, age 7. Harry turned 7 in August 1929, so the age fits exactly. In 1940 the same household has a Harry, 17, and no Roman, so they are the same boy. Children in Polish immigrant families often had one name at home and another at school. (A different, older Roman Trocki, born 1882 and married in Chicago in 1908, also shows up in the records. He may have been a relative of John.) Harry’s 1976 death record names his father as “Harry Trocki,” but that was almost certainly a mistake by whoever gave the details. The census and church records name him as John.

Across the ocean, in Wales

About four thousand miles away, a girl was born whose life would join Harry’s. Her parents, Sidney Isaac and Maggie (Margaret J.) Edwards, were married in the last months of 1914 in the Gower registration district, the countryside and villages just west of Swansea. Britain had been at war with Germany since August.

Their first child, William H. Isaac, was born in Gower in the spring of 1915. By 1917 the family had moved about ten miles west, across the Loughor estuary, to the Llanelli area, the tinplate town on the Carmarthenshire coast. That is where Dora Elaine Isaac was born on 18 March 1917. Her birth was registered in the Llanelly district (the old spelling) in the spring quarter of 1917, with her mother’s maiden name given as Edwards. Two more children followed there: David B. Isaac in early 1920 and Winifred M. Isaac in early 1924.

Dora’s family remembered her as being from Swansea, and in a sense she was. Gower, Llanelli and Swansea sit side by side around Swansea Bay, and she married in Swansea in 1944. Some records later call her “Dorrie.”

1917, not 1922: Dora’s American Social Security file gives her birth year as 1922, the same year as Harry. The Welsh birth index points to 1917 instead, and her 1946 arrival card agrees: it gives her age as 28, which is right for a woman born in March 1917. She was probably about five years older than Harry, and the American paperwork shaved those years off. Her birth certificate (Llanelly, vol. 11a, p. 2083) would settle it.
Which Sidney Isaac? (not yet proven) “Isaac” is a common surname in West Wales, and several Sidney Isaacs were born around Swansea in the 1890s. Two of them fit best:
  • Sidney Isaac of Dunvant, born about 1895–96, son of William and Elizabeth Isaac (listed in the 1901 and 1911 censuses). Dunvant lies in the Gower district, where Sidney and Maggie married. A Sidney Isaac born about 1895 died in mid-1960 and is buried at Killay, the next village to Dunvant. This is the stronger candidate.
  • Sidney Isaac of Burry Port, born about 1897, son of James and Elizabeth (Lizzie) Isaac. Burry Port is in the Llanelly district, where Dora, David and Winifred were born.

Two others can be ruled out. Sidney Russell Isaac married Margaret Olivia Lewis in 1922, and Sidney H. Isaac (1888–1940) was the son of David and Susannah Isaac. The 1914 marriage certificate (Gower, vol. 11a, p. 2274) gives Sidney’s age, job, address and his father’s name, which would show which Sidney he was. Margaret’s parents and her date of death are also still unknown.

1938–1945Loss, and then war

Bernice died on 21 July 1938, aged about 55, when Harry was fifteen. She was buried at St. Adalbert Catholic Cemetery in Niles, the great Polish cemetery on Chicago’s northwest edge. She died thirteen months before Germany invaded Poland.

Fourteen months later, on 4 October 1939, John remarried. His new wife was Marya (Mary) Kiljan, a widow from the same neighborhood (her story follows below). Eighteen months later the family suffered another loss: Harry’s brother Casimir died on 2 April 1941, aged just 26, and was buried at St. Adalbert near his mother.

Then America went to war. Harry registered for the draft in Chicago on 26 June 1942, and on 1 October 1942 he enlisted in the U.S. Army. That same year his father, now 61, filled out the “old man’s registration” that the government required of men born between 1877 and 1897. He gave his address as 2147 Dickens Avenue, wrote “unemployed,” and named Mary Trocki as the person who would always know where he was.

John died in Chicago on 1 July 1945, two months after the war in Europe ended, and was buried at St. Adalbert on 5 July. Harry was most likely still in uniform.

Mary’s story

Harry’s stepmother had a story of her own. Marya Faszczewska was born around 1881–82 near Warsaw, the daughter of Jacob Faszczewski and Catherine Stępańska. On 30 June 1901, in Warsaw, she married Władysław (Walter) Kiljan, a machinist. They had no children.

The Kiljans came to America much later than the Trockis. In August 1927, by then in their mid-forties, they sailed from the Free City of Danzig on the Danish liner SS Hellig Olav to New York. They settled at 1860 North Hoyne Avenue in Chicago, about three blocks from the Trockis on Dickens Avenue and almost certainly in the same Polish parish. Walter’s naturalization papers describe a big man, 200 pounds, gray-haired and blue-eyed, missing the tip of a finger on his right hand. He became a citizen in March 1938.

That summer brought two losses to the same few blocks. Bernice Trocki was buried at St. Adalbert on 25 July 1938. Walter Kiljan was buried in the same cemetery on 3 August 1938, nine days later. A little over a year after that, in October 1939, the widow and the widower married. Both were about 57, though Mary told the clerk she was 53. Mary became a citizen in her own right the same year, indexed as “Mary Kiljan or Trocki.”

Mary moved into 2147 Dickens with John and his grown children, Jennie, Walter and young Harry. Six years later she was widowed a second time. She stayed in Chicago, appearing in the 1950 census, and died on 16 February 1962. She was buried at St. Adalbert, the same cemetery as both of her husbands.

1942–1945Harry’s war

Harry registered for the draft on 26 June 1942 at Local Board 131 in Chicago. He was 19, still living with his father at 2147 West Dickens Avenue (telephone ARmitage 5439), and unemployed. The registrar described him as 5 feet 9 inches tall and 175 pounds, with blue eyes, brown hair, a light complexion and a scar on his right foot.

He was drafted and entered the U.S. Army in Chicago on 1 October 1942 as a private, Army serial number 36379309, enlisted “for the duration of the War plus six months.” His enlistment record lists him as single, with one year of high school.

Somewhere during his Army service, Harry met a young Welshwoman. No record found so far says how they met. But they were married in Swansea in the spring of 1944: the England and Wales marriage index lists Harry S. Trocki marrying a bride surnamed Isaac in the April–June quarter of 1944, Swansea district (vol. 11a, p. 1797). The index gives only the bride’s surname, but no other Trocki married in Wales in those years, so it is almost certainly Dora.

1943–1944Swansea at war

When Harry and Dora met, Swansea had already been through the worst of the war. Its docks, steelworks and oil refinery made it a target for the Luftwaffe, and in February 1941 three nights of bombing, the “Three Nights’ Blitz,” burned out the heart of the town. The Americans who arrived later found a battered city.

Then came the “friendly invasion.” In the twelve months before D-Day, almost 144,000 American soldiers and Navy construction troops landed in South Wales to build camps, depots and hospitals and to train for the invasion of France. The first complete U.S. division to arrive was the 28th Infantry Division, the Pennsylvania National Guard, which came in October 1943. Many of its men were descendants of Welsh immigrants to Pennsylvania, and its 109th Infantry Regiment was stationed across Glamorgan, Swansea’s county.

Swansea and the Gower Peninsula became part of the Army’s “Marshalling Area X.” Singleton Park, the big park on the west side of town, became Camp X3, a tented and hutted camp for up to 1,600 men of the 5th Engineer Special Brigade, the 12th Port of Embarkation and the 52nd Quartermaster Base Depot. The U.S. V Corps was based in the area. In spring 1944 the U.S. 2nd Infantry Division moved into Mumbles and Newton, at first living in St. Peter’s Church Hall and marching to Underhill Park for meals. Units trained on Gower beaches such as Oxwich and Horton, and invasion planning meetings were held at Penllergare House on the edge of the city.

For a young woman in Swansea the Americans were impossible to miss. They filled the streets, the dance halls and the cinemas. Some were stationed in local homes and church halls, and they brought chewing gum, nylons and big-band music to a town worn down by rationing and bombing. Romances were common, and many Swansea girls became GI brides.

In late May and early June 1944 the camps emptied. Troops from Gower camps such as Scurlage were trucked through the Uplands, down Walter Road and across the River Tawe into the sealed-off docks, and men of the 29th Infantry Division marched straight from Singleton Park to the ships. About 13,000 troops sailed from Swansea for Normandy, many of them bound for Omaha Beach.

From Swansea to Omaha Beach

Most of the combat units that had waited around Swansea went ashore at Omaha Beach, the most heavily defended of the five Normandy beaches.

  • V Corps, headquartered in the area, commanded the whole Omaha landing on 6 June 1944 under Major General Leonard T. Gerow.
  • 29th Infantry Division, the “Blue and Gray,” landed in the first assault waves on D-Day. Its 116th Infantry Regiment took some of the heaviest losses of the day.
  • 5th Engineer Special Brigade, which had camped in Singleton Park, also landed on D-Day. Its engineers cleared mines and beach obstacles under fire, marked lanes, and organized the beach so men, vehicles and supplies could keep coming ashore. The brigade ran Omaha Beach for months afterward.
  • 2nd Infantry Division, the “Indianhead” division billeted in Mumbles and Newton, came ashore at Omaha on 7 June, the day after D-Day, and pushed inland toward the hedgerow country around Saint-Lô.
  • 28th Infantry Division, the first American division in Glamorgan, was not in the landings. It reached Normandy in late July 1944 and went into battle in the hedgerows.

The port and supply units at Camp X3, including the 12th Port of Embarkation and the 52nd Quartermaster Base Depot, did their main work before the invasion: loading the ships and moving the mountain of equipment that went with the assault troops. Units like these followed to France in the weeks and months after D-Day to run the ports and depots that kept the armies supplied.

Harry’s unit has not yet been identified, so we cannot say for certain that he was one of them. But the dates fit. They married in Swansea between April and June 1944, the same weeks the camps were emptying for Normandy, and their son John was conceived around March 1944, when the American build-up around Swansea was at its height.

A memorial at Newton Village Hall in Mumbles honors the American forces who lived and trained in the area before leaving for Normandy. The 1940s Swansea Bay museum covers the town’s wartime years.

Their first child, John D. Trocki, was born in Wales in late 1944. His birth was registered in the October–December quarter, West Glamorgan district (vol. 11a, p. 1521), with his mother’s maiden name given as Isaac. That fits the arrival card, which says he was 16 months old in April 1946. Because his father was an American citizen, the baby was American too.

Spring 1946The crossing

Once the war was over, the U.S. government paid to bring the wives and children of its servicemen home. Under the War Brides Act of December 1945, the brides were let in outside the usual immigration quotas, and the great Cunard liners were refitted with cots, nurseries and extra laundries to carry mothers and babies instead of troops.

Most brides started at the U.S. Army transit camps near Southampton, the best known at Tidworth, where mothers and babies had medical exams and inoculations and their papers were checked. Then they went aboard the Queen Mary, still painted wartime gray, for a crossing of about five days.

Dora, now 29, was on board with her 16-month-old son John. The ship docked in New York on 4 April 1946. Her U.S. Immigration and Naturalization Service card records it plainly:

War brides didn’t go through Ellis Island. Immigration officers inspected them on board or at the pier, Pier 90 in Manhattan for the Cunard Queens. From New York, Dora and little John would have gone west to Chicago, probably by train, to start a life in a city she had never seen.

The shipAboard the “Grey Ghost”

The ship Dora sailed on had spent six years at war. Launched in 1936 as Cunard’s luxury flagship, the Queen Mary was painted battleship gray in 1940 and turned into a troopship. Her gray paint and her speed earned her the nickname the “Grey Ghost.” Over the war she carried roughly 810,000 servicemen, among them many of the American troops sent to Britain before D-Day.

When the fighting ended, the U.S. Army began what newspapers called “Operation Diaper”: bringing home the British wives and babies of American servicemen. In January 1946 the Queen Mary was refitted at Southampton for the job. A nursery and a playroom went in, extra laundries were added, and every cabin got cots. The troop bunks were pulled out of the first-class swimming pool, which became a drying room for diapers.

Before boarding

The Army ran two large camps near Southampton, the best known at Tidworth on Salisbury Plain. Brides from all over Britain, many of them from London on special trains out of Waterloo Station, gathered there for several days before sailing. They had medical exams and inoculations, their passports and visas were checked, and they sat through lectures on American life, money and slang. German prisoners of war still held in Britain carried luggage and did the camp chores. Dora would have made her way from South Wales to Southampton with a toddler in her arms.

The crossing

The crossing took four to five days. Compared with what their husbands had known on the same ship, the brides travelled in some comfort. Instead of thousands of GIs eating in shifts, the ship carried around 1,700 to 2,300 mothers and children on each run. American Red Cross nurses, Army welfare officers and baby specialists sailed with them. After six years of British rationing, the food was overwhelming. Many women had never seen so much or such variety, and plenty of them overate despite being warned.

On board, a daily newsletter called Wives Aweigh was written by the crew together with the brides. It carried world news, facts about the ship, cooking tips, poetry, and notes on the American towns the women were heading to. Cunard’s historian described it as a way to help prepare the brides for their new lives. One bride, June Allen, kept her copies and later gave them to the ship’s museum.

Arrival

The Cunard Queens tied up at Pier 90 on Manhattan’s West Side. When the first Queen Mary bride ship arrived that February, an Army band on the quayside welcomed the mothers with Brahms’ Lullaby and “Rock-a-Bye Baby.” Immigration officers did their checks on board and at the pier, and the women came down the gangways hundreds at a time, looking for husbands they hadn’t seen in months or even years. Some husbands met them in New York. Others waited at the end of long train rides across the country.

Across its war-bride voyages the Queen Mary carried about 12,886 brides and children to the United States and around 10,000 more to Canada, according to Cunard. Altogether, more than 100,000 spouses and children came to America under the War Brides Act, perhaps 60,000–70,000 of them from Britain.

What is and isn’t known about Dora’s own voyage: Her arrival card confirms the ship, the date (4 April 1946), that she was a war bride, and that John was with her. The details above describe the war-bride voyages in general. Nothing found so far records which camp she went through or what she remembered of the crossing. The full ship’s manifest (no. 9-19-15263) may add her Welsh address and her destination in Chicago.

The Queen Mary went back to passenger service in 1947. She retired in 1967 to Long Beach, California, where she is now a hotel and museum. Her Heritage Room shows video interviews with former crew, soldiers and war brides, and the WWII War Brides Association has held reunions on board.

1946–1960sA Chicago family

The family grew quickly. Harry Jr. was born on 23 December 1946, eight months after Dora landed. Paul Wayne followed on 10 December 1947. David Jerome and Michael Edward (born 1954) came later, making five sons in all.

They were a Polish-American Catholic family with a Welsh mother. By the 1970s Paul was living at 3554 South Union, in Bridgeport, the old neighborhood near the stockyards.

1974–1998Losses, and a long widowhood

The 1970s were hard. On 7 May 1974 Paul died in Chicago at just 26. He was single and working as a stock handler. He was buried at St. Adalbert, where his grandmother Bernice lay.

Two years later, on 28 March 1976, Harry died at 53. He too was buried at St. Adalbert in Niles.

Dora was a widow at 54, an ocean away from where she was born. She outlived her husband by more than twenty years. The family remembers her living in Chicago until about 1991. She died on 2 February 1998, aged 80 (75 by her American paperwork).

TodayWhat they left behind

Harry Jr. married Diane Smierciak and lived in Summit, Illinois. He died at home on 10 July 2022, survived by his son Ray and his grandchildren Elise and Lucas. Michael died in 2001, leaving his wife Sharon and their children Lisa, Jennifer and Michael. John, the baby who crossed the Atlantic on the Queen Mary, and David carry the family on. David’s daughter Rebecca is among Harry and Dora’s grandchildren.

Three generations on, the family runs back to a Welsh port, a Polish village near Suwałki, and a gray liner that crossed the Atlantic in the spring of 1946.
